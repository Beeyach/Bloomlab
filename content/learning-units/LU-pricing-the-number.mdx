## Start from what it costs, not from what they will pay

Most people price by guessing what the client will accept. That number tells you nothing about
whether the work is worth doing, and it is why so much agency work runs at a loss without anyone
noticing until they are tired.

Work the other way. Start with hours. Every line of scope is hours, and hours have a cost. Your
cost per hour is not your rate. It is what an hour of your working time actually costs you to
deliver: the software you pay for, the time you cannot sell to anyone else, the admin around the
work, the calls, the revisions.

For a solo operator that number is usually smaller than they think and larger than they hope.
Whatever yours is, write it down, and price from it.

<Callout kind="warning">
  A quote below your delivery cost is not a cheap job. It is a job you pay to do. Every extra hour
  makes it worse, and it is the only pricing mistake with no ceiling on how bad it gets.
</Callout>

## Margin, measured properly

Margin is what is left of the price after the cost of delivering it, as a share of the price.

Twenty-two hours at a delivery cost of $55 an hour is $1,210. Quote $2,200 and you keep $990,
which is 45% of $2,200. That is a 45% margin.

The mistake to avoid: dividing by the cost instead of the price. $990 on $1,210 of cost is 82%,
and that is a mark-up, not a margin. Both numbers are real, they are just answers to different
questions, and quoting the bigger one to yourself is a good way to feel healthy while running
thin.

Two more rules that keep margin honest:

- **Recurring revenue does not count toward the margin on a build.** A $200 a month retainer does
  not make a $1,300 quote on $1,210 of work into a good deal. It makes it a bad build attached to
  a decent retainer, and if they cancel in month three you have done forty hours for nothing.
- **Revisions and rush are hours.** Two rounds of revisions at two hours each is four hours you
  have promised. Include them in the cost before you look at the margin.

## Complexity is not the same as size

A big simple build is easier to price than a small complicated one. Complexity is what makes
hours unpredictable, and it comes from a short list of things:

- **Locations.** Two locations is not two calendars. It is two sets of hours, two sets of staff,
  routing between them, and a decision on every message about which address goes in it.
- **Integrations.** Every system you connect to is a system that can change without telling you.
- **Migration.** Moving existing data is the single most common source of "this took three times
  as long", because you find out what the data actually looks like halfway through.
- **Custom development.** Anything the platform does not do natively is work you own forever.
- **Number of people who have to use it.** A system one person uses can be strange. A system six
  people use has to be obvious.

Complexity moves the hours. It does not move the rate. If you find yourself adding a "difficulty
premium" on top of hours you have already counted, you have counted the hours wrong.

## Risk is the part you cannot see yet

Risk is different from complexity. Complexity is work you can see and count. Risk is the chance
that something you have not seen adds work.

A client who has been burned by an agency before is risk. A client whose data lives in a system
you have never used is risk. A deadline set by something outside the project, like a shop
opening, is risk, because when it slips you cannot move it.

The way to price risk is a contingency: a percentage on top of your cost, sized to how uncertain
you are. Five percent per point on a one to five scale is a reasonable starting shape. A quiet,
well-organised client with clean data might be a 1. A rushed two-location migration for someone
who has been let down before is a 4.

<Depth title="Why a contingency is not padding">
  Padding is adding money because you are nervous. A contingency is a number you can explain: this
  build has three things in it that historically add time, and here is what that has cost on past
  jobs. The difference matters because a client will ask, and "because it might take longer" is an
  answer that invites negotiation while "migrations from Square have added four to six hours on
  every one I have done" is not.
</Depth>

## The minimum viable version

Every project has a smallest version that still solves the problem. Knowing it is what lets you
stay in a conversation when the budget is real and lower than your quote.

The minimum viable version is not "the same thing but cheaper". It is a smaller promise. Work it
out by asking what the client's actual problem is, then keeping only what is needed to change it.

For Marcus, the problem is that he takes calls with people who will never buy. The smallest thing
that changes that is an application form with qualification routing on it. Not the calendar, not
the no-show recovery, not the migration. Those all matter, and none of them is the problem he
described.

Two things make this honest:

1. **The price comes down with the scope.** If you cut the scope and hold the price, that is not a
   smaller version, it is a worse deal, and they will work it out.
2. **You say plainly what they are not getting.** "This version does not move your Typeform
   questions, so you will retype them" is a sentence a client can decide on. Silence is not.

<Callout kind="idea">
  If you cannot describe the minimum viable version of a project in one sentence, you do not yet
  understand what the client is buying.
</Callout>

## Putting it together

For any build:

1. List the scope. Put hours on every line.
2. Multiply by your delivery cost per hour. That is the floor, and nothing goes below it.
3. Add a contingency sized to the risk you can actually name.
4. Pick a price that leaves a margin you can work at, and check it against the price, not the cost.
5. Write down the smallest honest version, in case you need it.

There is no single correct price at the end of that. There is a range of defensible ones, and a
clear line under which none of them sit.
